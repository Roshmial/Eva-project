# Slide shapes and evidence

## Executive summary

Use: context → management problem → offered capability → value/status → conditions → requested decision.

Detailed cases belong later. Keep technical model-quality metrics away from the headline value metric unless they influence the executive decision.

## Role-value slide

Show several practical benefits or decision levers for every role. Use one shared economic model as the connective tissue. Put diagnostic questions in speaker notes.

## Mechanism slide

Explain how information becomes a decision, for example:

demand → service and SLA → dependencies → resources and consumption → cost drivers → scenario and decision.

## Case slide

Use three blocks:

1. Situation — observable starting condition and decision pressure.
2. What was done — concrete analysis, linkage, calculation, or decision.
3. What was obtained — decision and result, with status.

A roadmap, application portfolio, target model, or other deliverable is not a case by itself. Reframe it around the client situation that required a decision, then show how the artifact supported that decision. A generic case can describe a systematic scan across a whole perimeter. A specific case should name the service, quantities, actions, and measured result. Keep both only when the distinction is visible on the slide.

## Diagnostic maturity sales slide

Use a four-part executive hierarchy:

1. One aggregate score or status for orientation; never let the average hide domain constraints.
2. A domain profile on one common scale, preferably as a row-based scorecard for five to seven dimensions.
3. Current level, target level, and visible gap for every domain; keep target and peer benchmark as separate concepts.
4. A decision layer: strong areas, limiting gaps, prioritized actions, and the next step.

A row-based scorecard or heatmap is the safest default for a sales slide because it shows the deliverable directly and remains legible across five to seven domains. A radar is acceptable for a compact five-domain executive profile, but use short labels and no more than three contours: current, target, and a real benchmark. A radial map of blocks explains methodology coverage and interdependence; it does not by itself show maturity or the management decision.

Before interviews, either leave the scorecard unfilled or label every filled profile `illustrative example — not a company assessment`. Do not invent an aggregate score from placeholder domain values. Show percentile or peer position only when the assessment has a documented comparative base.

Named companies may illustrate publicly documented practices, but place them in a separate benchmark strip and state that this is not a formal maturity rating. Avoid implying that a logo belongs to an exact level without a published assessment.

## Pilot and full model

Pilot outputs can include current service cost, service-resource-consumer links, data availability, and calculation rules.

Full-model outputs can add deeper allocation, planning and forecasting, scenario TCO, internal tariffs, and management P&L where internal pricing and accounting policy permit.

## Scaling by taxonomy

Use the Solutions layer when selling broader applicability. Do not list applications, licenses, personnel, and hardware as peer solution clusters. When an offer combines cloud and infrastructure across several taxonomy objects, call it an offering-specific cross-cutting scope or supra-cluster and state that it is not a standard taxonomy object.

## Metric status language

- Realized: “сэкономили”, “снизили” — implementation and result are confirmed.
- Historical: identify the source context and avoid presenting it as a new-client forecast.
- Calculated: “расчётный эффект” or “оценённый эффект”.
- Potential: “целевой ориентир” or “гипотеза предложения”.

Never add separate percentages unless the source explicitly states that they are additive.
