# Purpose

This skill governs how to produce a serious Russian market study on AI solutions available in Russia.

The goal is not to restate the user's own outline more elegantly. The goal is to turn a rough brief into a real consulting-style paper: market picture, product classes, buyer logic, implementation barriers, trends, examples, and a defensible source trail.

# When to use

Use this when the user asks for:
- a market overview of AI products available in Russia;
- a technology radar of product classes;
- a comparison of Russian enterprise AI vendors or platforms;
- a client-ready draft with evidence, examples, and recommendations;
- a Russia-only view, where foreign-market background is irrelevant unless explicitly requested for contrast.

Do not use this for:
- a short shortlist only;
- a single-vendor due diligence memo;
- a generic global AI market overview.

# Core delivery standard

1. Build a real paper, not a reformatted brief.
   - Do not just repeat the user's own trend hypotheses and problem statements.
   - Expand with additional confirmed signals from public sources: market numbers, survey results, adoption evidence, deployment patterns, and named examples.

2. Keep the report alive and explanatory.
   - Major sections should be written as analytical prose in Russian, not as long flat bullet dumps.
   - Bullets and tables are for structure, not for replacing analysis.
   - Every major section should answer not just `what exists`, but `why this matters for buyer choice`.

3. Inline-source the important claims.
   - For key theses, put the source link directly in the paragraph where the claim is made.
   - A final source list is necessary but not sufficient.
   - If a paragraph contains a number, market share, survey result, or policy claim, that paragraph should visibly point to its source.

4. Distinguish three evidence levels.
   - Confirmed fact: directly supported by a public source.
   - Interpretation: your conclusion based on several facts.
   - Unknown: not publicly disclosed; mark it explicitly.

5. Remove decorative AI language.
   - Strip generic statements like `AI is changing everything`, `the future belongs to AI`, `new level of innovation`, unless tied to a concrete mechanism, forecast, or example.
   - Replace AI-shaped filler with operational meaning: process acceleration, document throughput, integration cost, deployment constraint, data quality, security model, staffing need, measurable effect.

# Recommended structure for this class of report

1. Executive summary
   - 4-8 dense paragraphs.
   - Answer: how the Russian market is structured, what classes exist, where the market is already usable, where risk is overstated, and what companies usually get wrong.

2. Scope and methodology
   - State that the study is Russia-only.
   - Explain what came from the user input table and what came from external public sources.
   - State which criteria are publicly observable and which require RFI/demo.

3. Market picture
   - Give market sizing and growth indicators only from named public sources.
   - Add at least one adoption or budget signal, not just product-count commentary.
   - Add institutional context when relevant: state strategy, national projects, sector priorities.

4. Technology radar by product class
   - Explain each class in buyer language: what task it solves, when to choose it, what it does not solve.
   - Keep unlike classes separate: universal assistant/agent platforms, embedded enterprise AI, specialized AI, RPA + AI, full-cycle AI/ML platforms, chat/voice interfaces.

5. Representative vendor set
   - Select a balanced set across classes, not ten near-clones.
   - For each system: what it is, where it fits, where it is strong, where it is constrained.
   - Do not pretend public marketing pages are enough to score deep security/governance features.

6. Implementation barriers
   - Must include more than the user's own seed list.
   - Cover: data quality, process maturity, integration landscape, infosec/closed contour requirements, operating model, staffing, economics, model control, adoption by business users.
   - Support the barriers with surveys, interviews, or market examples where possible.

7. Trends to 2030
   - Avoid futurist fluff.
   - Tie the optimism section to concrete drivers: institutional support, deployment economics, local models, closed-contour demand, sector adoption, migration from pilots to embedded operational use.

8. Practical buyer logic
   - End with a decision-oriented map: what class of solution to choose for which kind of business request.

# Writing rules for Misha

- Keep the prose in Russian.
- English terms are allowed only where they are standard and useful: RAG, RPA, OCR, IDP, low-code, on-premise, private cloud, API, SDK, LLM.
- Avoid English connective scaffolding and decorative business words when there is a clear Russian equivalent.
- The paper should sound like an experienced consultant, not like a trend article or a vendor brochure.

# Pitfalls

1. Repeating the user's hypotheses as if they were already researched.
   - Fix: add external evidence, counterweights, and extra market signals.

2. Hiding all sources at the end.
   - Fix: place links next to important claims in the body.

3. Over-scoring vendors on weak public evidence.
   - Fix: if deployment/security/licensing detail is missing, label it as unknown and move on.

4. Turning the document into generic AI commentary.
   - Fix: every abstract statement must resolve into a business mechanism, a market example, a number, or a decision implication.

5. Writing a vendor list instead of a market study.
   - Fix: keep the center of gravity on classes, buyer logic, barriers, and implementation reality.

# Verification checklist

- [ ] The report is Russia-only unless the user explicitly asked for comparison with other countries.
- [ ] Important numbers and survey claims have inline links in the relevant paragraph.
- [ ] The narrative adds external evidence beyond the user's original prompt.
- [ ] Product classes are explained in buyer language, not only by technical taxonomy.
- [ ] The barriers section is concrete and supported, not a generic list.
- [ ] The trends section is optimistic but grounded, not promotional.
- [ ] The final section tells a company what class of solution to choose in which case.
